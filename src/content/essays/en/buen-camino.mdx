I recently watched a film called [<ruby>The Way<rp>(</rp><rt>朝圣之路</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Way_(2010_film)).

It does not tell a complicated story: a father, seeking to fulfill his late son's unfinished wish, sets out alone on the [<ruby>Camino de Santiago<rp>(</rp><rt>圣雅各之路</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camino_de_Santiago). Precisely because it is so simple, it strikes the heart all the more deeply. There is no deliberate sentimentality, only a man setting out with his grief on his back—walking through wind and rain, and slowly learning, in the company of strangers, how to live with loss.

The road itself has an almost epic quality. It begins in [<ruby>Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port<rp>(</rp><rt>圣让-皮耶-德-波尔</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port), France, and ends in [<ruby>Santiago de Compostela<rp>(</rp><rt>圣地亚哥-德-孔波斯特拉</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiago_de_Compostela), Spain. Along the way are ancient <ruby>monasteries<rp>(</rp><rt>修道院</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>, stone-built towns, and vast plains, as well as mountain paths in the morning mist and bells ringing at dusk. It is, of course, a deeply religious <ruby>pilgrimage route<rp>(</rp><rt>朝圣路线</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>, but today it feels more like a road leading inward: people set out not necessarily to reach a miracle, but more often to make their way through themselves.

## One Road, Two Kinds of Arrival

What moved me most about the film was not only the scenery, but its understanding of <ruby>walking<rp>(</rp><rt>行走</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>. We tend to think that moving forward means solving, proving, and arriving. In this film, however, walking is more like a slow process of digestion. Through the steps repeated day after day, a person lays grief bare, lets go of attachment, and gradually returns unspeakable emotions to time and the body.

In this way, the meaning of <ruby>pilgrimage<rp>(</rp><rt>朝圣</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby> also becomes more subtle. It does not necessarily promise a radiant destination; it is more like a quiet yet resolute act of self-repair. You set out carrying your fractures, and the road will not immediately mend everything for you. But it will help you understand that people do not gain the strength to keep living only after they have completely healed. Often, it is precisely in the process of continuing to live that a wound slowly grows new boundaries.

Some lines are like milestones by the roadside: they seem quiet, yet linger in the mind for a long time.

> *"Have you ever walked the Camino, señora?"*
> "Have you ever walked the <ruby>Camino<rp>(</rp><rt>圣路</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>, ma'am?"
>
> *"When I was younger, I was too busy. And now that I'm old, I'm too tired."*
> "When I was young, I was always too busy; and now that I'm old, I'm too tired."

These lines read almost like a footnote to so many lives. When we are young, we always imagine there will be plenty of time. Once this busy stretch is over, we tell ourselves, we will go see the sea, travel far away, and do what we truly want to do. But life often does not grant us a respectable “later.” Once many things are missed, they can only return to us as memories in some quiet moment.

> *"There's a whole world out there to beat you up plenty. You don't need to go looking for people to pile on."*
> "Life already beats us up enough; don't let certain people make it worse."

There is an almost rough-hewn tenderness in these words. In life, misunderstanding, disappointment, illness, and separation are already unavoidable. To willingly hand yourself over to depletion and malice on top of all that would be far too unworthy. The ability to treat one another with even a little more kindness is already something rare.

> *"You don't choose a life, Dad. You live one."*
> "Life is not something to be chosen, but something to be experienced."

This line is almost the key to the entire film. We cling to thoughts of “if only I had” and “what if I had chosen another road,” but what truly makes up a life has never been those hypotheticals. It is the reality you are passing through here and now. Life is not a problem waiting for an optimal solution; it is more like a stretch of road whose waters you must wade through yourself.

I later thought that perhaps this film moved me because it was never speaking only of Spain's famous <ruby>Camino<rp>(</rp><rt>圣路</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>. It speaks of the stretch of every person's life that must be traversed alone. You may never actually shoulder a backpack and set out, cross the Pyrenees at dawn, or enter a church in a foreign land at dusk. But there will certainly be a period in your life that you must face alone, digest alone, and move through alone.

Some people walk the road that comes after parting; some walk the road that follows self-doubt; and some walk the road of slowly withdrawing from an old life and then, little by little, growing a new order. Those roads may come with no <ruby>credential<rp>(</rp><rt>朝圣证书</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>, no cathedral at the destination, and no exuberant applause upon “arrival,” but they are no less solemn.

## Conclusion

If there is something truly moving about the phrase [<ruby>Buen Camino<rp>(</rp><rt>一路顺风，愿你走在好路上</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/buen_camino), it is perhaps that it urges no one to arrive faster, nor does it demand that anyone become more remarkable. It simply offers you a gentle blessing: may you continue along your own road in safety, with clarity and honesty.

May we all, on our own <ruby>pilgrimages<rp>(</rp><rt>朝圣之路</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>, reconcile with loss, recognize ourselves, and rediscover the meaning of life through the long journey on foot.

<ruby>Buen Camino<rp>(</rp><rt>Buen Camino</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>.
