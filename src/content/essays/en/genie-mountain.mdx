## I. Alone: The Road to the Sunrise-Gilded Peaks (August 1)

It is now 21:32. I have completed the first day of my trek around <ruby>Genie Mountain<rp>(</rp><rt>Genie</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>, and I am lying in my tent at the <ruby>Sunrise-Gilded Peaks<rp>(</rp><rt>Rizhao Jinshan</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby> campsite, ready to write down today's story.

The weather was against us when we set out in the morning, with a steady drizzle falling. We agreed to "use technology" on the first day: let a vehicle carry our luggage while we walked with light packs. The scenery was so spectacular that every few steps we could not resist stopping to take photos. Before we knew it, it was already noon, and we had only just reached the first plateau.

Sisi suggested calling a motorcycle and leaving a phone number, but the drivers here did not take numbers; they said we could simply flag one down if we met one along the way. Fair enough. We kept walking and taking photos. At one in the afternoon, Sisi suggested that we just take a vehicle, and everyone tacitly agreed. But I still wanted to try. After wrestling with the decision for a while, I made up my mind: <strong>I was going to walk.</strong> I could not say whether it was stubbornness or an unwillingness to give in. I had come all this way, so I had to measure this road with my own feet. Sisi and the others were concerned about me too, and told me to take a vehicle if I had not arrived by six. I said okay. In truth, I was not confident either, but I simply wanted to walk it. Sisi gave me a bottle of water, an energy gel, and some bread. And with that, I set out on a journey of my own.

First came the first <ruby>mountain pass<rp>(</rp><rt>Pass</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>. I made it up and even overtook quite a few people along the way—though of course I was carrying a light pack, so it was not a fair comparison. Following the direction everyone else was going, I glanced instinctively at my track: wrong, I had drifted off course. After thinking for two seconds, I decided to follow the track. I did not know where their road led, but it was not part of my route. And so an "abstract" stretch of tracking began.

I took a look and saw that the quickest way back was to head straight down the mountain. Good grief—there was no path at all. Never mind; I just had to walk. I instinctively looked back. Not a single person had come this way. In that moment, I was a little frightened, but even more than that, I felt a strange excitement—_as though the whole mountain belonged to me._ <strong>It's all right. This is passable. I can do it.</strong> Following the track, I began cutting upward. The tire marks along the way became my motivation, as though telling me: if a vehicle can make it through, then it can be walked; I will definitely be able to cut back to the route. After wading through water and crossing the mountain, I finally returned to the line. But looking around, there was no trace of a path anywhere—only boundless grassland and high mountains. There was no one behind me, not even a single yak. It seemed that I was the only person left between heaven and earth. But there was no alternative. The campsite lay ahead, and I had to keep going. The solitude frightened me, yet it also made me more clear-headed than ever before—<strong>out here, no one but you can take even a single step on your behalf.</strong>

Without realizing it, I walked into a thicket. There was no path at all; I could only force my way through the gaps. At last I cut my way out and saw two motorcycles in the distance. My heart finally settled. Following the motorcycles' direction, I traced the route to a narrow path beside a stream. The path was so narrow that I thought carrying a heavy pack here would pose a real risk of falling, and I could not help feeling fortunate. Beyond the path, a flat area dotted with structures resembling yurts came into view. To make sure I had not gone the wrong way, I went over and asked how far it was to the Sunrise-Gilded Peaks campsite. A young man kindly replied, "Five or six kilometers. You can cross the little bridge here, then climb up on the gentler slope on the other side." I thanked him at once, feeling much more reassured. At least it meant I had not gone astray. This was the right path, and it would take me to the campsite.

There was one troubling detail: this entire stretch was an ascent. Alone, I felt as though I had returned to the pilgrimage circuit around [<ruby>Mount Kailash<rp>(</rp><rt>Mount Kailash</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Kailash), except that this time I had no one with me on the trail, no support, and no oxygen. The weather kept worsening. I had to put on my raincoat and begin crossing a pass more than 4,000 meters above sea level. I kept my eyes fixed on my phone, terrified that one wrong step would leave me trapped in the mountains—or even dead there. That thought was not an exaggeration: in a place like this, a single mistake can be fatal. Mild hypothermia began to set in. My fingers stiffened, and inside my raincoat I could no longer tell sweat from rain. But I did not stop. A little hypothermia was all right; it was not my first time. You can do this. Checking the route every few steps kept tempting me to look into the distance and wonder how much farther I had to go and when the crossing would end. I knew that was bad; it was wearing away my patience. I could not continue like that. I had to focus steadily on each step beneath my feet. I began to wonder: why do people come to places like this to suffer? But then I thought—perhaps it is precisely this kind of "hardship" that makes the comfortable routines of city life feel precious. To keep my direction true, I began using distinctive shrubs as markers, checking how far I had drifted and how much distance remained each time I reached one. At last, I made it up. Still not another person in sight, not a single <ruby>yak<rp>(</rp><rt>Yak</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>. Standing on the pass, panting hard while wind-driven rain struck my face, I could not help smiling—**I had crossed another mountain.**

> Out here, no one but you can take even a single step on your behalf.

Fortunately, I finally had a little signal. I estimated the campsite's position and gained a rough sense of its direction and distance. It was nearly four in the afternoon, and I felt that this downhill stretch should get me there quickly. I messaged my teammates to tell them I was safe, and they asked for my location. I continued on and encountered a herd of cattle. I was delighted: animals meant there ought to be a proper path nearby. At last, as I kept descending, I saw a road marked by tire tracks, and it aligned with my route. This was it. This was the main road to the campsite. Humming a song, I quickened my pace, looking forward to reuniting with my teammates.

At last, the outline of the campsite appeared ahead. But there was no signal—the farther down I went, the less signal I had. I stared blankly at the packs around me and searched everywhere for my teammates, but found no sign of them. There happened to be a small shop nearby, so I decided to try my luck; perhaps they were there. They were not. After a short rest, I began searching for a place with reception so I could contact them, worried as well about whether they had any signal. Finally, I reached the hilltop and found reception. A few minutes later, a message from my teammates came through, and we reunited. We pitched the tents and boiled water. I only half knew what I was doing, but fortunately they helped me. At last, we were settled.

But today felt like a narrow escape from death. Curled up alone in the tent while rain continued falling outside, I was unhappy and feeling a little
emo. I even wanted to go home. Looking back over the journey—getting lost, wading through water, crossing mountains, hypothermia—any one of those would have been enough to deal with. But then I thought: *Aren't these exactly why I came here? If everything went perfectly smoothly, how would it be any different from lying at home scrolling through my phone?*
But it is all right. **Come on, Lele—you can do this!**

See you tomorrow.

## II. Together: From New Lenggu Monastery to the "Slope Campsite" (August 2)

It was my first time camping in the wild. Rain fell throughout the night, and the ground where we had pitched our tents was sloped, so I kept sliding downward in my sleep. Just as I finally began to feel drowsy, a sudden "roar—" sounded outside the tent, like some kind of wild beast, and jolted me awake. Images flashed through my mind: was it a bear? How terrifying. There were no human voices outside, only the intermittent "roar—". My teammates were awake too. When I learned that yaks were wandering around the tents, my heart calmed slightly. But I still could not sleep. I worried that one might come over, ram my tent, or step on me, which made sleep even more impossible. I could not resist quietly lifting one corner of the tent to sneak a look. It was pitch-black. The low, thunderous "moo—" rang out from time to time, but it was farther away than I had imagined, and nothing could be seen clearly in the darkness.

At last, dawn came, and the rain eased a little. I thought I would go to the toilet. Yesterday I had glimpsed a small toilet hut at the campsite; surely it would be better than relieving myself outdoors. When I got there—good grief, someone was inside, and there was neither a curtain nor a door. Someone emerged from the second stall. I asked, "Is anyone in there?" "No, but you can't use it." A bad feeling rose within me. I stepped inside and found a ghastly scene—feces everywhere, with nowhere to put my feet. I tried with great difficulty, but it was impossible. I managed to urinate and fled at once.

Back at the tent, Sisi and A-Yin had not yet risen. I thought about eating something, but did not want to boil water; it was too much trouble. I would make do with a few snacks. A protein bar barely filled my stomach. It was so difficult: there was a pile of gear to pack, and today I had to carry everything along the route. Just thinking about it made my head ache. There was no alternative. Time to pack.

I opened the tent and looked at the scenery outside—so beautiful. Clouds and mist curled around the mountainsides like a fairyland. I remembered the line: "I have a one-square-meter bedroom and a 510-million-square-kilometer living room." In that moment, all the disarray and exhaustion were washed clean by the view before me. Somehow, it all felt worthwhile again.
